The ML/AI engineer interview playbook

ML, AI-engineer and SWE loops look different on the job post, but they're built from the same six rounds. Prep the shared core once, then add the layer the role needs.

You asked what you should actually know about these interviews. Short answer: six round types, each testing something specific. You already have strong raw material for most of them. What's usually missing is packaging: practising out loud, structured answers, and stories with numbers.

Not every company runs every round. Startups often swap the OA and DSA for a take-home or a practical pairing session. Big companies usually keep the DSA rounds.

1. The rounds: what each one really tests

Round What they test What a strong answer looks like Your edge
Online assessment (OA) Speed and correctness on 2-3 timed problems, sometimes MCQs Clean, passing solutions with edge cases handled LeetCode Knight / CF Specialist history
DSA / live coding Problem solving while communicating Clarify → brute force → optimise → code → test, narrating throughout Same, now in Python
ML fundamentals Do you understand the models and metrics you use? Crisp definitions plus when it matters in practice You run experiments and ship models
ML / LLM system design Turning a fuzzy product goal into a working, measurable system The structured framework below, with explicit trade-offs Java system design + real pipelines
Project deep dive Did you build it, and do you understand the decisions? Problem, constraints, your choices, alternatives rejected, measured result, what you'd change An agent, an LLM pipeline, a recommender, an experiment
Behavioral / HM Ownership, collaboration, judgement, motivation Specific STAR stories, no blaming Real stakeholder friction to draw on (framed well)
Take-home Practical engineering quality Working code, tests, a README with decisions and limitations, on time Exactly what notes 02–05 build toward

ML fundamentals checklist (know these well enough to explain)

  • Bias-variance, over- and underfitting, regularisation (L1/L2, dropout, early stopping)
  • Train/validation/test splits, cross-validation, time-based splits, data leakage
  • Metrics: precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC (imbalance), calibration, RMSE/MAE; ranking metrics (recall@k, NDCG, MAP)
  • Logistic regression, tree ensembles (random forest vs gradient boosting), k-means and its assumptions
  • Embeddings, the transformer at a high level (tokenisation, attention, context window), fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting
  • Recommenders: collaborative filtering, matrix factorisation, hybrid models (LightFM is one), cold start, implicit feedback
  • Experimentation: hypothesis tests, p-values and what they're not, power and sample size, multiple testing, variance reduction (e.g. CUPED), novelty effects, network effects and interference

2. Prepping with limited time

  • Breadth before depth. One pass over every round type beats perfecting DSA while system design stays at zero.
  • Out loud, under time. Interviews test performance as well as knowledge. Do at least one mock per week (friends, peers, or a platform like Pramp or interviewing.io).
  • Reuse your work. Every system design practice can be a variation of something you built: the agent, the review pipeline, the recommender.
  • Keep an error log. One line per mistake ("forgot empty input", "didn't define the online metric"). Read it before each interview.
  • Protect sleep and weekends. A plan you can sustain for 6 weeks beats a 2-week sprint followed by burnout. Work-life balance is the whole point of the switch.

3. A framework for ML / LLM system design answers

  1. Clarify (3-5 min). Who uses it, what decision it drives, rough scale (QPS, catalogue size, docs), latency budget, cost limits, privacy constraints. Write the assumptions down.
  2. Frame it. Classification, ranking, retrieval-plus-generation, or forecasting? Define a business metric (e.g. resolution rate, conversion), an offline metric (recall@k, NDCG, faithfulness) and guardrail metrics (latency, cost, complaints).
  3. Data. Where labels come from (explicit, implicit, human-labelled, LLM-labelled and then audited), freshness, leakage risks.
  4. Baseline first. A heuristic or simple model, then something better, and explain why the step up is worth it.
  5. Serving. Batch vs real-time, caching, feature store or not, fallbacks when the model or LLM is down.
  6. Evaluation. An offline set, then an online A/B test with a clear success criterion. This is your home turf.
  7. Monitoring. Data and prediction drift, quality sampling, cost and latency dashboards, feedback loops.
  8. Trade-offs. Say them out loud: quality vs latency vs cost, build vs buy, and what you'd cut for v1.

Worked example A: a RAG support assistant for a food-delivery app

Prompt: "Design an assistant that answers customer questions about orders, refunds and policies."

  • Clarify: customers in the app, in English and Hindi. It answers policy questions and shows order status. Risky actions (refunds) are escalated to humans, not automated, in v1. Responses should feel interactive, so the answer streams.
  • Metrics: business is the self-serve resolution rate and fewer escalations. Offline: retrieval recall@5, answer faithfulness, correct refusals. Guardrails: p95 latency, cost per conversation, complaint rate.
  • Architecture:
  • Data: policy docs chunked by section with metadata (region, effective date). Order data is reached only via tools scoped to the logged-in user, never pasted into the index.
  • Evaluation: a golden set from real tickets (policy, status, out-of-scope and injection cases). Gate retrieval and faithfulness in CI, then A/B test against the existing flow on resolution rate.
  • Risks and trade-offs: hallucinated policy (mitigated by grounding, citations and an "I don't know" path), prompt injection through user text, stale docs (re-index on change), and cost (small model for routing, cache common answers).

Worked example B: a "recommended for you" menu recommender

Prompt: "Recommend items on the home screen of a food-ordering app."

  • Clarify: per-user and per-store menu, a tight latency budget on app open, availability changes during the day.
  • Frame it: a two-stage system. Candidate generation (recall: a few hundred items) feeds a ranker (precision: the top 10). Business metric: add-to-cart rate or conversion. Offline: recall@k for candidates, NDCG for the ranker. Guardrails: latency, diversity, margin.
  • Data: implicit feedback (views, add-to-cart, orders), item metadata, context (time of day, store, weather if available).
  • Models: popularity per store and time slot as the baseline. Then matrix factorisation or a hybrid model like LightFM, which uses item and user features and so helps with cold start. A learning-to-rank model (e.g. gradient-boosted trees) can rerank using context features.
  • Serving: precompute candidates in batch (your scheduled reruns), rank online with fresh context, filter out unavailable items, and fall back to popularity.
  • Evaluation: offline metrics on a time-based split (no leakage from the future), then an A/B test. Watch for position bias and feedback loops, where the system only learns about items it already shows, and keep a little exploration.

4. Project deep dives: a STAR template with numbers

Prepare 3 stories, 2-3 minutes each, that you can then expand under questioning: the agent, the LLM review pipeline, and the experiment.

Part What to say Prompt to yourself
Situation Business context in one sentence Who hurt, and why did it matter?
Task Your specific responsibility What was mine?
Action 3-4 decisions with the alternatives you rejected Why this design? What did I try first?
Result A measured outcome plus what you learned What number moved? What would I change now?

Example skeleton for the experiment story: "Leadership wanted to know whether a wallet cashback actually drove extra orders or just subsidised orders that would have happened anyway (S). I owned the experiment design and the measurement pipeline (T). I set up stratified control and treatment groups, plus [your integrity and balance checks, and how KPIs were computed] (A). The read showed an estimated impact of ₹3.5 Cr+. Next time I'd add a pre-registered power analysis and CUPED to tighten the intervals (R)."

Expect follow-ups: How did you randomise? How did you check balance? What would invalidate the result? What's the confidence interval? For the agent: How did you stop bad SQL? How did you evaluate it? What did it cost per query? Practise those out loud.

Behavioral tips. Have stories ready for: a conflict with a stakeholder, a failure, a time you pushed back, something you owned end to end, and learning something fast. Talk about non-technical managers constructively: "I learned to show results as a decision plus its confidence, not raw tables." Never badmouth your current employer, since interviewers read it as a preview of how you'd talk about them. For "why switch?", be forward-looking: "I want to build and own ML systems end to end, with engineering practices like version control, tests and evals."

5. India-specific things to expect

  • OA platforms: HackerRank is very common. HackerEarth, Codility, CodeSignal, Mercer Mettl and iMocha also show up. They're typically timed, with hidden test cases, so practise in a plain editor with a timer and get used to writing your own input parsing when the problem needs it.
  • Notice period comes up early, often in the very first call: "What's your notice period? Is it negotiable? Can you join early?" Many roles prefer early joiners. Check your offer letter or HR policy for your exact notice period (it can differ during probation) and whether early release or buyout is possible. Answer factually: "My notice is N days; I can explore early release."
  • CTC questions: "current CTC" and "expected CTC". Know your breakdown (fixed vs variable vs other components), and anchor expectations on the role and market, not just your current number. For startups, ask about ESOP terms (vesting schedule, cliff, exercise window) and treat ESOPs as uncertain value.
  • Do you hold other offers? Be honest. A competing offer is legitimate leverage, but don't invent one.
  • Channels: referrals tend to get faster responses than cold applications. Startups recruit heavily via Wellfound, Instahyre, Cutshort, LinkedIn and founders' posts. Naukri remains widely used by larger companies and recruiters.
  • Background verification after an offer is standard. Keep your dates, titles and documents consistent across your resume and LinkedIn.

Weekdays are 45 minutes. Saturdays are longer (about 2 hours), and Sundays are for rest plus a 30-minute review. It builds on the week 1 plan you already have.

From week 3 on, start applying to roles you'd like but aren't your top picks, so the real interviews double as practice before your dream ones.

Common interview questions

  • "Walk me through your resume / tell me about yourself." 90 seconds: present → past → why this role. Lead with what you built.
  • "Why move from data science to ML engineering?" Forward-looking: owning systems end to end, with engineering rigour (version control, tests, evals, deployment).
  • "Design a RAG assistant / a recommender / a fraud model." Use the 8-step framework, state the metrics early, and talk trade-offs.
  • "How did you evaluate your agent?" Golden set, tool-call accuracy, task success, cost and latency, plus how failures became new test cases.
  • "Explain precision vs recall and when you'd favour each." Tie it to costs: missing fraud vs annoying customers.
  • "How do you know your A/B result is real?" Randomisation and balance checks, power, pre-defined metrics, no peeking, confidence intervals, guardrails.
  • "What's your notice period / expected CTC?" Factual and calm: "N days, early release possible. Expecting a range in line with the role."

15-min try this

  1. Set a timer for 5 minutes and write your 90-second intro: current role, 2 things you built with a result each, and why ML/AI engineering next.
  2. Set 10 minutes and fill the STAR table for your agent project. In Action, list 3 decisions, each with one rejected alternative.
  3. Read both out loud once, timed. Cut anything that runs past 2 minutes.
  4. Save them as interview/stories.md in your notes repo. You'll refine them every week.

You don't need to know everything. You need a repeatable way to answer, stories with numbers, and enough practice that nerves don't wipe your memory. Six weeks at 45 minutes a day is enough to get there.
